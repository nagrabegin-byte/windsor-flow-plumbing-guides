Freeze-Proofing Your Windsor Home: A Room-by-Room Checklist Before the First Hard Freeze

Winter in Windsor arrives fast. One week the evenings are cool, the next the overnight low drops well below freezing, and any water sitting still in an exposed pipe is at risk. A frozen pipe rarely announces itself — the first sign is often a faucet that gives nothing, and the second is the water that escapes when the ice thaws and the pipe splits.

The good news is that most freeze damage is preventable with a walk through the house, room by room, before the first hard freeze.

Start outside: hose bibs, sprinkler lines, and the garage.

Begin where the cold hits first. Disconnect every garden hose from its outdoor faucet and store it for the season — a hose left connected traps water in the faucet and the pipe behind it. Shut off the water to each exterior faucet from the valve inside the house, then open the outdoor faucet to let the remaining water drain out.

Sprinkler lines need the same treatment: a blowout clears them with compressed air so nothing is left to freeze and crack heads and pipes. In the garage, check the door seal — a gap at the bottom lets cold air pour in around water lines in the walls. Pipes in an unheated garage should be insulated.

The kitchen: sinks on exterior walls.

Kitchen sinks often sit against an outside wall, which makes the pipes behind them the coldest in the house. On the coldest nights, open the cabinet doors under the sink so warm room air can reach the pipes. If the sink is on a north-facing wall, this small habit matters more than it sounds.

Seal gaps where pipes pass through the wall — cold air through a small hole can freeze a pipe that would otherwise be fine. Know where the shutoff valves under the sink are, and test that they turn; a valve that has not moved in years can seize when you need it most.

Bathrooms: the coldest rooms in the house.

Bathrooms combine several freeze risks: pipes in exterior walls, fixtures that sit unused for hours, and tile and stone surfaces that hold the cold. Keep bathroom doors open during cold snaps so heat circulates, and let a faucet drip slightly on the coldest nights — moving water is much harder to freeze than still water, and the cost of a slow drip is far less than the cost of a burst pipe.

Look under each vanity for drafts around the pipes and seal them. If a bathroom is rarely used, do not shut its heat off entirely — a guest bath kept at a low, steady temperature is far safer than one left cold.

The basement and utility room: the heart of the system.

The basement is where most of the system lives, and a little preparation there pays off most. Insulate exposed water lines, especially those running along outside walls or near windows. Pay attention to the main water shutoff — every adult in the house should know where it is and how to turn it, because shutting off the water quickly is the single most useful thing you can do when a pipe bursts.

Check the water heater for drafts and keep the area around it clear. If the home has a sump pump, make sure its discharge line runs well away from the house and is not blocked — a frozen discharge line can back water up into the basement. And look for small leaks now: a drip that seems harmless in October becomes a frozen blockage in January.

Laundry room and mudroom: small rooms, real risk.

Laundry rooms and mudrooms are often the least heated spaces in the house, and they hold water lines all the same. Washer supply hoses should be checked for cracks and bulges, and replaced if they show wear — a failed hose floods a room in minutes. If the laundry room sits over an unheated garage or crawl space, the pipes below it deserve insulation.

In the mudroom, any sink or hose connection needs the same treatment as an outdoor faucet: drain it and shut it off for the season.

Leaving town: the away-from-home plan.

A home left empty in winter is the most vulnerable of all, because no one is there to notice a problem. If you travel, keep the heat on — never below a steady low setting — and ask someone to check the house every day or two. Shutting off the main water supply before a long trip removes the risk almost entirely; if you do, drain the lines by opening the lowest faucet in the house after the shutoff.

Leave cabinet doors open under sinks on exterior walls, and make sure the person checking the house knows where the main shutoff is.
