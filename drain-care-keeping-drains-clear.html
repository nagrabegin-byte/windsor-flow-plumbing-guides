Drain Care: Keeping Your Windsor Home's Drains Clear All Year

Drains only get attention when they stop working. Until then they take everything a household sends down them without complaint — grease from the pan, hair from the shower, soap scum, coffee grounds, and the odd bit of food that slipped past the strainer. The trouble is that drains keep a record of all of it. What goes down does not always go away, and a slow drain is usually a clog that has been building for months.

Good drain care is not complicated. It is mostly a set of small habits that keep the buildup from starting, plus knowing the difference between a drain you can help yourself and one that needs a professional.

The kitchen drain: grease is the enemy.

Grease is the most common cause of kitchen clogs, and it causes them in a way that surprises people. Hot grease poured down the drain looks harmless — it is liquid, after all. But it cools as it travels, coats the inside of the pipe, and hardens into a sticky layer that catches everything else. Over time that layer narrows the pipe until water barely passes.

The fix is simple: never pour cooking grease down the drain. Let it cool in the pan, scrape it into the trash, and wipe the pan with a paper towel before washing. A sink strainer catches food scraps that would otherwise add to the buildup, and emptying it into the trash takes seconds. Running cold water while the disposal is on helps move ground-up food through the line instead of letting it settle.

Bathroom drains: hair, soap, and slow buildup.

Bathroom drains clog differently. Hair is the main culprit — it wraps around the stopper and the drain crosspiece, and soap scum binds it into a mat that water cannot get through. The first sign is a shower that drains slowly and leaves you standing in a puddle, or a sink that takes its time emptying.

A drain screen over the shower and tub drains catches most hair before it goes down, and cleaning the screen weekly is far easier than clearing the pipe later. Once a month, lift the sink stopper and pull out any hair and buildup you can reach — wear gloves, and you will be glad you did. Avoid pouring strong chemical cleaners down the drain: they can damage pipes and fixtures, and they rarely clear a real clog anyway.

What never to put down any drain.

Some things do not belong in any drain, no matter how small the amount. Cooking oil and grease, coffee grounds, eggshells, pasta and rice that swell with water, and fibrous foods like celery and potato peels all cause trouble in kitchen lines. In the bathroom, wipes — even the ones labeled flushable — do not break down the way toilet paper does, and they are a leading cause of blockages in home and city sewer lines alike.

Medications, paint, and household chemicals should never go down a drain either. They do not just risk a clog; they can harm the plumbing and the water system beyond the house. When in doubt, the trash is the safer choice.

A monthly habit that actually helps.

Once a month, give every drain a little attention. Pour a kettle of hot — not boiling — water down the kitchen drain to soften any grease film, and run hot water through the bathroom drains for a minute. Check that strainers and screens are clean and in place. Look under sinks for dampness or a musty smell, which can mean a trap is leaking or a drain is starting to back up.

These small checks take ten minutes and catch most problems while they are still small. A drain that is slowing down a little each month is telling you something; a drain that stops all at once has been telling you for a while.

When a slow drain means something bigger.

Some slow drains are local — one sink, one shower, one fix. Others point to a problem deeper in the system. If several drains slow down at the same time, if water backs up into a tub when the toilet flushes, or if a drain gurgles when another fixture is used, the blockage is probably in a shared line or the main sewer line. That is not a job for a plunger or a drain snake from the hardware store.

Recurring clogs in the same drain are another warning sign. A clog that keeps coming back after clearing usually means the pipe itself has a problem — a buildup that a snake cannot reach, a pipe that has sagged and holds water, or roots that have found their way into a sewer line.
