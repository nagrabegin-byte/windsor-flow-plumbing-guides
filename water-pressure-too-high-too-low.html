Water Pressure Too High, Too Low — and What It Means for Your Home

Water pressure is one of those things you only notice when it is wrong. Too low, and the shower is a trickle, the dishwasher takes forever to fill, and the upstairs faucet barely runs. Too high, and the pipes bang when a tap shuts off, the toilet fill valve hisses, and fittings wear out years early. Both problems are worth understanding, because pressure that is wrong in either direction quietly damages the plumbing.

Most homes are happiest with pressure somewhere around 40 to 60 pounds per square inch. Below that range, daily life gets annoying. Above it, the system is under stress it was not built to carry. The first step is finding out where your home sits.

How to check your pressure at home.

A pressure gauge costs little at a hardware store and screws onto any outdoor hose faucet. Attach it, make sure no water is running anywhere in the house, and open the faucet fully. The needle shows the static pressure — the pressure in the system at rest. Write the number down.

For a fuller picture, check it at two different times of day. Pressure often runs higher at night, when fewer homes on the street are using water, and lower in the morning, when everyone is showering. If the reading is steady and in range, the system is probably fine. If it swings widely, or sits well outside the comfortable range, something upstream needs a look.

Too low: the usual suspects.

Low pressure has several common causes, and they are worth checking in order. Start with the main shutoff valve: if it is partly closed, every fixture in the house suffers. A valve that was turned during a repair and never fully reopened is a frequent culprit. The same applies to the shutoff valves at individual fixtures — a half-closed valve under a sink starves that tap alone.

Next, look at the pipes. In older homes, mineral scale narrows the inside of the pipes over years, and the flow drops gradually until someone notices the shower has gone weak. A pressure-reducing valve that has failed can also choke the supply. And a leak in the main line — even a small one underground — can bleed off pressure before the water reaches the house, often with a soggy patch of yard as the only sign.

Too high: the quiet pipe-killer.

High pressure is less obvious and more damaging. It does not announce itself with a weak shower; it shows up as banging pipes when a tap shuts off, a toilet that keeps running, a water heater relief valve that drips, or a washing machine hose that bursts without warning. Every fixture and joint in the house is rated for a certain pressure, and running above it wears them all out faster.

The usual cause is a failed or missing pressure-reducing valve — the device on the main line that steps the street pressure down to a safe level for the house. Street pressure in many areas runs well above what a home needs, and without a working reducing valve, the full force reaches every tap. A valve that has stuck open, or a home that never had one, will show the symptoms across the whole house at once.

When pressure swings from room to room.

Pressure that is fine in the kitchen but weak upstairs, or strong in one bathroom and thin in another, usually points to something local. A clogged aerator on a faucet — the small screen at the tip — can cut flow at a single tap, and cleaning it takes a minute. A partially blocked supply line, a failing fixture, or a valve that is not fully open can do the same.

If the difference is between floors, gravity and pipe condition both play a part. Water loses pressure as it climbs, so a home with marginal pressure will always feel it worst on the top floor. That is not necessarily a problem with the pipes — but if the top floor used to be fine and has gotten worse, the pipes may be narrowing with age.

What we check first.

Diagnosing pressure is a process of elimination, and it starts at the main line: the shutoff, the pressure-reducing valve, and the condition of the supply line from the street. A reading at the hose faucet tells us the system pressure; a reading at individual fixtures tells us where the pressure is being lost. From there, the fix depends on what we find — a valve adjustment, a failed reducing valve, a clogged line, or a supply line that has reached the end of its life.

Do not ignore pressure that has changed. A drop that appeared over weeks can mean a leak is growing somewhere you cannot see, and a rise can mean a valve has failed and the whole system is now running hot.
