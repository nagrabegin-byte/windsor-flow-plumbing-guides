How to Flush Your Water Heater — DIY Steps and When to Call a Plumber

A water heater does its work quietly, and most homeowners only think about it when the hot water runs out or the tank starts rumbling. That rumbling is sediment — minerals and grit that settle at the bottom of the tank every time water is heated. In Windsor, where the water carries a fair amount of dissolved minerals, sediment builds up faster than many homeowners expect, and a yearly flush is the simplest way to keep the heater running well.

Flushing is a job many homeowners can do themselves in about an hour — but it involves hot water, pressure, and, for gas heaters, an open flame. Know the steps, know the limits, and know when to hand it over.

What sediment does to a heater.

Every gallon of water brings a small amount of dissolved minerals into the tank. Heat makes those minerals drop out and settle at the bottom as a layer of grit. That layer sits between the burner or heating element and the water, so the heater works harder and longer to heat the same amount of water. Energy use climbs, parts wear faster, and the tank ages before its time.

The symptoms show up gradually: popping or rumbling as trapped water boils under the sediment, hot water running out sooner than it used to, and a tank needing attention years earlier than expected. A heater that has never been flushed in a hard-water home can hold several inches of sediment — and then a flush alone may not be enough.

Before you start: safety and supplies.

You will need a garden hose long enough to reach a drain or a safe spot outside, a pair of work gloves, and a bucket. For an electric heater, turn off the power at the breaker before you touch anything — never work on a heater that is still powered. For a gas heater, turn the gas control to the pilot setting or off, following the label on the unit.

Let the water cool for a few hours before you start — a full tank can scald, and the drain valve releases water under pressure. If the heater is leaking, the drain valve is damaged, or you are unsure which valve is which, stop and call for help instead of guessing.

Flushing a tank water heater, step by step.

First, shut off the cold water supply to the heater — the valve is on the cold inlet pipe at the top. Then open a hot water faucet somewhere in the house, which lets air into the system so the tank can drain. Connect the garden hose to the drain valve near the bottom of the tank and run the other end to a floor drain or outside, somewhere the hot water will not harm plants or people.

Open the drain valve and let the tank empty. Watch what comes out: clear water at first, then cloudy water and grit as the sediment leaves. Once the flow slows, briefly open the cold water supply again in short bursts — they stir up the sediment at the bottom and push it out through the hose. Repeat until the water runs clear.

Close the drain valve, remove the hose, close the hot water faucet you opened, and turn the cold supply back on. Let the tank fill completely before restoring power or relighting the burner — running a heater with a partly empty tank can damage it — then check the drain valve and all connections for drips.

Tankless? Descaling is the flush.

A tankless water heater has no tank to drain, but it still needs regular care. Its narrow heat exchanger passages scale up with mineral deposits, and a scaled-up exchanger overheats, shuts down, or throws error codes. Descaling a tankless unit means circulating a cleaning solution through the exchanger with a small pump, following the maker's instructions for the right solution and the right interval.

Many homeowners leave this one to a professional — it needs the pump, the correct solution, and a careful flush afterward so no cleaning solution stays in the water lines. If a tankless unit in a hard-water home has never been descaled, the interval in the manual is a good place to start.

Signs the flush is not enough.

A flush helps a heater that is in good shape. It does not rescue one that is failing. If the drain valve will not open, if the water coming out is rusty, if the tank leaks from the bottom, or if the heater is old and has never been flushed, the sediment may have hardened into a solid layer that will not wash out. A heater that rumbles loudly even after a flush, or that runs out of hot water quickly despite a full tank, is telling you its useful life is near the end.

In those cases the honest question is whether more maintenance is worth it, or whether the money is better spent on a replacement.
